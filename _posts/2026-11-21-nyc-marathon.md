On November 1, I ran the TCS New York City Marathon. Bib 60400. My first marathon, running for Baruch and for Bangladesh.

It was also a record day. 61,146 people finished, the first time any marathon in the world has passed 60,000 finishers. I'm one of them.

## Two years to get here

This race didn't start on race day. I got my spot through NYRR's 9+1 program, which means running nine qualifying New York Road Runners races and volunteering at one more. Along the way I ran the NYC Half Marathon in about 2:20, which until then was the farthest I had ever raced.

So by the night before, I had two years of running behind me, and I was still nervous.

## Race morning

I woke up, made my way down to the ferry and rode across the harbor to Staten Island, past the Statue of Liberty, on a boat full of runners.

{% include photos.html a="/assets/writing/2026-11-nyc-marathon/statue-of-liberty.webp" a_alt="The Statue of Liberty seen from the water on a clear morning" caption="On the ferry to the start." %}

Every runner starts on Staten Island, and mile one is the climb up the Verrazzano-Narrows Bridge, with thousands of people around you and Brooklyn straight ahead. The overhead sign usually points drivers to JFK. That morning it said "MTA B&T welcomes you to NYC Marathon."

{% include photos.html a="/assets/writing/2026-11-nyc-marathon/verrazzano.webp" a_alt="Runners crossing the Verrazzano-Narrows Bridge under a sign welcoming them to the NYC Marathon" caption="The Verrazzano-Narrows Bridge. Mile one." %}

## The city shows up

Then the city takes over. Brooklyn, Queens, the Bronx and Manhattan line the streets the whole way, just to cheer for strangers. I lost count of the high fives somewhere in the hundreds, plus more hugs than I can remember.

The signs were their own kind of fuel: "WE LOVE HILLS," with a picture of the Queensboro Bridge on it, "You paid money for this?!" and "Where is everyone going?"

{% include photos.html a="/assets/writing/2026-11-nyc-marathon/signs.webp" a_alt="Spectators holding signs that say We Love Hills, You paid money for this, and Where is everyone going" caption="Fair questions, honestly." %}

## Past mile 18

My longest run before race day was 18 miles. Everything after that marker was new: every step from there was the farthest I had ever run, and I felt every one of them.

## The Fifth Avenue wall

Everyone warns you about Fifth Avenue, and they are right. After the Bronx you come back into Manhattan and the road tilts up, a long, slow climb at mile 23 when your legs have nothing left. It was brutal.

It's not just me, either. Across the 2026 field, the climb up Fifth Avenue at mile 23 was where runners lost the most time: the median runner ran it 16.5% slower than their first half.

{% include photos.html a="/assets/writing/2026-11-nyc-marathon/running-1.webp" a_alt="Zayeed smiling while running, wearing bib 60400 and a hydration vest" b="/assets/writing/2026-11-nyc-marathon/running-2.webp" b_alt="Zayeed running among other marathoners under yellow fall trees" caption="Still smiling, somehow." %}

Then you turn into Central Park, the trees have just turned for fall, the crowd gets louder, and the finish line is finally close.

## The finish

I crossed the line in just around five hours. By the time I made it out of the park it was dark, and I ended up flat on the ground with the Bangladesh flag over me and the medal on my chest. That photo says it better than I can.

{% include photos.html a="/assets/writing/2026-11-nyc-marathon/finish-flag.webp" a_alt="Zayeed lying on the street after the race, wrapped in the Bangladesh flag with his finisher medal" caption="Done." %}

My friends and family were out there for me all day, and they were waiting at the end in the cold. Thank you for being there.

{% include photos.html a="/assets/writing/2026-11-nyc-marathon/finish-friend.webp" a_alt="Zayeed in the orange finisher poncho with his medal, next to a friend" b="/assets/writing/2026-11-nyc-marathon/finish-crew.webp" b_alt="Zayeed with four friends after the race, one of them biting the medal" caption="The crew." %}

One more thing I loved about the day: the men's race was won by Andreas Almgren of Sweden, in his marathon debut, and he has a background in data science. Two first marathons on the same course that day. His was about three hours faster.

## What's next

I want to run all of the World Marathon Majors, starting with Chicago and Boston. And next time the goal is simple: train for pace, and go sub-4.

It was a memorable day. 26.2 miles, five boroughs, one medal, and a crew that waited for me in the dark.
